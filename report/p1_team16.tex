\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This report investigates a Sugeno (Takagi--Sugeno) \cite{takagi1985fuzzy}
fuzzy PD controller that
balances an inverted pendulum mounted on a cart on a \SI{30}{\degree} slope.
The plant is the same as in Lab~2 but with different cart mass, pendulum mass
and pendulum length, and the controller is implemented as a 5$\times$5 rule
base with crisp consequents instead of a Mamdani inference system.

The control objective is stated directly in terms of closed-loop response
characteristics:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
  \item settling time $\ts \le \SI{1}{\s}$ for every initial angle
        $\gam_0 \in \{-10\degC, -5\degC, 5\degC, 10\degC\}$;
  \item negligible overshoot $\overshoot$ in $\gam$;
  \item steady-state angle as close to zero as possible;
  \item negligible oscillation in all responses.
\end{enumerate}
Cart displacement and cart velocity are explicitly outside the scope of the
assessment.

The weight force acting down the slope is the disturbance the controller must
compensate for at steady state,
\begin{equation}
  W = g \sin\phi \, (m_c + m_p),
  \label{eq:weight}
\end{equation}
which is used as a tuning reference: the fuzzy output at zero error must be
able to generate a force of at least this magnitude, otherwise the pendulum
cannot be held off-centre on the slope.

\section{Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

The controller is a fuzzy PD law~\cite{astrom2006feedback} with the desired state $(\gam^\ast, \gamdot^\ast) = (0,0)$:
\begin{align}
  p &= \gam^\ast - \gam = -\gam, & d &= \gamdot^\ast - \gamdot = -\gamdot, \\
  f &= \mathrm{evalfis}(\mathrm{fis}, [p, d]), & f &\leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(f, -50, 50).
\end{align}
The output is saturated at $\pm\SI{50}{\N}$ to mimic the actuator limit of the
real system. The proportional error supplies the restoring effort and the
derivative error provides damping; because both inputs are fed through the same
rule table, the effective closed-loop behaviour is that of a non-linear PD
controller whose gains vary over the operating region.

\subsection{Performance metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

All comparisons in this report use the same four metrics, each computed from
the simulated $\gam(t)$ and $f(t)$:
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
  \item[Settling time $\ts$] the first time after which $|\gam|$ stays within
        the $\pm\SI{1}{\degree}$ band for the remainder of the response.
  \item[Overshoot $\overshoot$] the maximum excursion of $\gam$ beyond zero in
        the direction opposite to $\gam_0$, normalised by $|\gam_0|$.
  \item[Steady-state error $\sse$] mean of $|\gam(t)|$ over the last
        \SI{1}{\s} of the simulation.
  \item[Late-time variation] peak-to-peak range over the final \SI{1}{\s},
        measured separately for $\gam$, $\gamdot$ and $f$. This includes slow
        drift as well as oscillation. The final-second error and ranges are
        finite-horizon measures, not proof of an asymptotic steady state.
\end{description}

\section{Tuning Investigations}
\label{sec:tuning}

\paragraph{How to read this section.}

\subsection{Method A: Gravity-compensated output membership values}
\label{sec:method-a}

\paragraph{Motivation and hypothesis.}
At $p=d=0$, only the $(\mathrm{ZO},\mathrm{ZO})\to\mathrm{ZO}$ rule fires,
so the baseline produces zero force. Holding the system stationary on the
slope instead requires $W=\SI{12.979}{\N}$ from \eqref{eq:weight}.
Biasing the output membership values towards this force should reduce the
angle error needed to generate an uphill force.

\paragraph{Design.}
Each baseline output singleton $F_i^{(0)}\in\{-50,-25,0,25,50\}\,\si{\N}$
is replaced by
\begin{equation}
  F_i(k)=\min\!\left(50,\max\!\left(-50,F_i^{(0)}+kW\right)\right).
  \label{eq:method-a-bias}
\end{equation}
The input membership functions, 25 rules, inference methods and simulator
remain unchanged. Ten bias fractions are tested at all four starting angles.
Table~\ref{tab:method-a} shows representative settings; the full sweep is in
Appendix~\ref{app:method-a-data}. Selection minimises the worst-case
final-second mean absolute angle error, rather than settling time alone.

Before clipping, the Sugeno weighted average satisfies
$\sum_r w_r(F_r^{(0)}+kW)/\sum_r w_r=f_0(p,d)+kW$.
Thus the bias shifts the control surface upwards. Clipping the PB singleton
at \SI{50}{\N} reduces that shift where PB-output rules fire; the implemented
method is not identical to adding a constant after fuzzy inference.

\paragraph{Results and interpretation.}
Increasing $k$ from zero to one reduces the residual error and allows all four
responses to settle within \SI{0.5}{\s}. This supports the hypothesis that the
missing uphill bias contributes to the baseline offset. The best tested
setting for the selected error criterion is $k=1.05$, giving
\begin{equation*}
  [F_{\mathrm{NB}},F_{\mathrm{NS}},F_{\mathrm{ZO}},F_{\mathrm{PS}},F_{\mathrm{PB}}]
  =[-36.372,-11.372,13.628,38.628,50.000]\,\si{\N}.
\end{equation*}
It reduces the worst-case residual error by \SI{92.9}{\%} relative to the
baseline. The worst final-second angular-velocity and force ranges also fall,
from \SI{0.00978}{\degree\per\s} and \SI{0.9738}{\N} to
\SI{0.000643}{\degree\per\s} and \SI{0.0671}{\N}, respectively.
Figure~\ref{fig:method-a-comparison} compares the complete responses.

The choice involves a trade-off: $k=1$ has lower overshoot and a slightly

the stationary force balance. Although the selected setting meets the
settling-time target, its \SI{24.38}{\%} worst-case overshoot and nonzero
residual error do not meet the no-overshoot and zero-error targets.

\subsection{Method B: Asymmetric output-singleton spacing}
\label{sec:method-b}

\paragraph{Motivation and hypothesis.}
This method adjusts the force requested by the baseline NS and PS
consequents. Since the slope makes
uphill and downhill motion unequal, independently tuning these forces may
improve the correction and braking response.

\paragraph{Design.}
The output singletons are parameterised as
\begin{equation}
 [F_{\mathrm{NB}},F_{\mathrm{NS}},F_{\mathrm{ZO}},F_{\mathrm{PS}},F_{\mathrm{PB}}]
 =[-50,-F_-,0,F_+,50]\,\si{\N},\qquad 0<F_-,F_+<50.
 \label{eq:method-b-spacing}
\end{equation}
The baseline has $F_-=F_+=25$. Input MFs, rules, inference methods and the
simulator remain unchanged. Unlike Method A, this method changes output
spacing without shifting the zero singleton; the two methods are not combined.

A $9\times9$ grid, $F_-,F_+\in\{5,10,\ldots,45\}$, followed by refinement
around both the overall and symmetric selections tests 193 distinct pairs
at four initial angles, giving 772 simulations. Of these pairs, 80 give
valid responses that all settle within \SI{1}{\s}. The selected pair has
the lowest worst-case absolute overshoot among these,
$O_\gamma=\max_t\{0,-\operatorname{sgn}(\gam_0)\gam(t)\}$;
ties are resolved by settling time and integral absolute error.
Here $O_\gamma$ is in degrees, rather than the percentage metric $\overshoot$.

\paragraph{Results and interpretation.}
The selected outputs are $[-50,-40,0,49.5,50]\,\si{\N}$.
All four responses settle within \SI{0.37}{\s}, meeting the stricter
below-\SI{0.5}{\s} target, whereas the baseline does not settle within
\SI{5}{\s} for every start. Compared with baseline, worst-case overshoot
falls from \SI{2.996}{\degree} to \SI{1.123}{\degree}, residual error
from \SI{1.943}{\degree} to \SI{0.854}{\degree}, and final-second
force range from \SI{0.974}{\N} to \SI{0.408}{\N}.
This smoother final response comes with a small increase in total force
variation, $\sum_i|f_{i+1}-f_i|$, from \SI{129.1}{\N} to \SI{132.2}{\N}.

Increasing the intermediate singleton magnitudes strengthens corrective
forces without narrowing the input MFs. Most of the improvement is already
obtained with symmetric spacing. Asymmetry adds a smaller benefit: reducing

\SI{1.123}{\degree} at the same settling time. Figures~\ref{fig:method-b-heatmaps}

Figure~\ref{fig:method-b-params} shows the membership functions and control surface.

The selected $F_+=49.5$ lies at the tested upper boundary, close to the PB

still cannot supply the stationary uphill holding force. Final-second

\subsection{Method C: Rule weighting}
\label{sec:method-c}

\paragraph{Motivation and hypothesis.}
At $p=d=0$ only the $(\mathrm{ZO},\mathrm{ZO})\to\mathrm{ZO}$ rule fires
(its neighbours are triangular, not flat-topped, so they vanish exactly at
the origin), forcing $f=0$ whenever $\gam=0$; this is the same root cause
Method A addresses, but here the input MFs and all 25 crisp consequents are
left untouched. Away from the origin, 2--4 rules overlap. The hypothesis is
that giving each rule its own weight $w_{ij}\in(0.02,1]$ can bias which
overlapping rule dominates the \texttt{wtaver} blend enough to pull the
equilibrium and the transient response closer to the targets, without moving
any breakpoint or output level.

\paragraph{Design.}
Each rule's \texttt{addRule} weight is one entry of a $5\times5$ matrix
$W$ (rows/cols ordered NB,NS,ZO,PS,PB for $p,d$), giving
\begin{equation}
  f = \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\min(\mu_p^i,\mu_d^j)\,z_{ij}}
           {\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\min(\mu_p^i,\mu_d^j)}.
  \label{eq:method-c-weight}
\end{equation}
$W$ is searched with \texttt{fminsearch} (derivative-free, since the cost
comes from the simulator, not a closed form) from several random and
warm-started restarts, minimising an integral-squared-error cost on $\gam(t)$
(weighted after $t=\SI{0.5}{\s}$ and over the final \SI{1}{\s}) plus explicit
overshoot and $\sse$ penalties, summed over all four $\gam_0$. A first
optimised setting (S2) is reported alongside the adopted one (S3), which
adds two extra penalty terms, $\sum(\Delta f)^2$ and $\sum(\Delta\gamdot)^2$
restricted to the second half of the run, because S2 is a negative result
(Section~\ref{sec:negative}) despite its tracking numbers.

\paragraph{Results and interpretation.}
Rule weighting alone takes worst-case $\ts$ from $\infty$ to
\SI{0.40}{\s} and worst-case $\sse$ from \SI{1.943}{\degree} to
\SI{0.604}{\degree} (S3), by shifting the equilibrium blend rather than any
breakpoint. Neither zero overshoot nor zero $\sse$ is reached: the rule
active at the exact origin still always outputs $0\,$N regardless of its
weight, so weighting can only push the equilibrium close to $\gam=0$, not
onto it (Section~\ref{sec:combination} considers combining this with
Method A/B's output shift, which can move that zero-force point).

S2 minimises $\ts$, overshoot and $\sse$ further than S3, but its worst-case
force range over the final second is \SI{34.185}{\N} against S3's
\SI{0.893}{\N} -- a $38\times$ difference -- and its worst-case
$\gamdot$ range is \SI{12.945}{\degree\per\s} against S3's
\SI{0.291}{\degree\per\s}. Several $w_{ij}$ in S2 sit at the $0.02$ floor,
so \eqref{eq:method-c-weight} effectively switches between near-binary force
levels as $p,d$ cross a rule boundary, i.e.\ bang-bang control. S3 trades
$\sim\SI{0.27}{\degree}$ of extra $\sse$ and higher overshoot for removing
this chatter, judged necessary because ``negligible oscillation'' is an
explicit requirement. Figure~\ref{fig:methodc} shows the adopted S3 responses.

\subsection{Method D: ZO dead-zone width}
\label{sec:method-d}

\paragraph{Motivation and hypothesis.}
Method C diagnosed the baseline offset as the single
$(\mathrm{ZO},\mathrm{ZO})\to\mathrm{ZO}$ rule always forcing $f=0$ at the
origin. Rule weighting cannot change that rule's own output; instead this
method narrows the ZO term's half-width on both inputs (crisp outputs and
rules untouched, isolating this lever from A/B/C), so a smaller angle error
is enough to bring a non-zero rule into play and the controller starts
correcting sooner.

\paragraph{Design.}
The ZO half-widths $z_p$ (baseline \SI{2.6}{\degree}) and $z_d$ (baseline
\SI{35}{\degree\per\s}) parameterise all five terms per input symmetrically,
e.g.\ $P_{\mathrm{ZO}}=[-z_p,0,0,z_p]$, $P_{\mathrm{NS}}=[-10,-z_p,-z_p,0]$,
with the outer NB/PB breakpoints fixed, so the partition still exactly tiles
the universe. $(z_p,z_d)$ were tuned by a 2D grid search, then a continuous
\texttt{fminsearch} refinement, over worst-case $\ts$, $\overshoot$, $\sse$
and final-second force range across all four $\gam_0$. A first setting
minimised these well but rang visibly (Section~\ref{sec:negative}): the
search only scored the final second of each run, so it never penalised
transient wobbling. The reported setting adds a ringing penalty -- the
number of $\gamdot$ sign changes over the \emph{whole} run beyond the one
expected reversal on the way to the target -- and re-searches.

\paragraph{Results and interpretation.}
Narrowing the ZO region alone takes worst-case $\ts$ from $\infty$ to
\SI{0.50}{\s} and worst-case $\sse$ from \SI{1.943}{\degree} to
\SI{0.712}{\degree}, with every response showing exactly one clean
overshoot-and-settle. \SI{0.50}{\s} is a boundary result, not comfortably
under the target: both the wide grid and the continuous refinement converge
to the same point, so this appears to be a genuine limit of tuning
$z_p,z_d$ alone rather than a search shortfall -- narrowing further keeps
lowering $\ts$ but reintroduces ringing (Table~\ref{tab:method-d}, row 2).
Worst-case overshoot falls only modestly, to \SI{42.76}{\%}: shrinking the
ZO region also shrinks the distance over which the NS/PS ramps act, raising
the effective gain near the origin, which speeds convergence but also makes
overshoot more likely. As with Method C, the residual $\sse$ persists because
$z_p,z_d$ do not change what the origin rule itself outputs. Unlike Method
C, no force chattering appears at any tested setting (the levers Method C
needed to touch -- crisp outputs, rule weights -- are both left at baseline
here). Figure~\ref{fig:methodd} shows the adopted responses.
